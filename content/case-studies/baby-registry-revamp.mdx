      ### A Registry That Missed the Mark

      Walmart had redesigned its baby registry just a year prior, but the new version wasn’t performing as hoped. The team’s original hypothesis was that expecting parents would appreciate a pre-populated registry filled with recommended products. In practice, the opposite proved true. Baby registries are deeply personal, and parents-to-be want to curate their own lists, choosing items that reflect their needs, preferences, and budgets. Instead of feeling helpful, a “fully loaded” registry felt impersonal and overwhelming, leading to low engagement and limited adoption.

      ### The Challenge at Hand

      We were tasked with fixing this. In just one quarter, our team needed to design, develop, and launch an improved baby registry experience that would resonate with expecting parents while supporting Walmart’s business goals. The problem wasn’t just cosmetic; it was fundamental. We had to rethink the role of the registry, moving from a static, pre-built list to a tool that invited participation, felt personal, and provided real value during a major life event.

      ### The Team and Stakeholders

      This was a large, multi-disciplinary effort. The core team included design, product management, and three engineering teams spanning iOS, Android, and Web. We had support from analytics, research, and our business partners, most notably the head merchant for the baby category. Our primary stakeholder was the VP and head of the baby category, who was deeply invested in seeing registry performance improve. While design and product leadership supported the effort, they were largely focused on other high-profile initiatives at the time, giving our team unusual autonomy and accountability.

      ### Why It Mattered

      The baby registry wasn’t just another feature; it was a strategic touchpoint for Walmart. Registries often serve as the entry point to a long-term relationship with new parents, driving not just initial purchases but ongoing loyalty across multiple categories. Getting it wrong meant missing a critical opportunity. Getting it right could create lasting connections with families at a pivotal moment in their lives.

      ### Goals

      * Increase quality registry creations (creations that lead to a first curation action)
      * Increase curation by 10%
      * Increase sharing by 20%
      * Increase purchase conversion by 25%
      * Helping new parents with this major moment in life

      ### A Tight Timeline

      We had just six weeks to take the project from discovery through final deliverables, with much of that time falling in the holiday-heavy back half of Q4. I knew we needed to move quickly and assemble a team that could hit the ground running.

      ### Clarifying Scope and Resourcing Needs

      After aligning with our business and product partners, I had enough clarity on the scope to start making staffing decisions. One of my staff designers was well-positioned to lead the work, but I also knew that expecting him to carry the full load would put both the team and the project at risk.

      ### Building a Team Creatively

      Rather than waiting for headcount or formal allocations, I looked for creative ways to build the right team. The wedding registry team at Walmart was entering a period of uncertainty due to pending org changes, leaving some talented designers underutilized. I made the case to design leadership to temporarily borrow a couple of those designers, and supplemented the rest of the team with strong contractors from our existing pool.

      ### A Nimble, Cross-Functional Approach

      This approach allowed us to act fast, stay nimble, and build a cross-functional team that blended deep domain knowledge with fresh perspectives, all without missing a beat on our timeline.

      ### Leveraging Existing Research

      The Routine Consumables team was responsible for the baby category at Walmart, and fortunately, the registry had undergone a design overhaul just a couple of years prior. That meant we had a strong foundation of research to build from: market studies, customer feedback, and interview data that offered rich insights.

      ### Grounding the Team in User Insights

      Understanding users’ motivations, sources of friction, and moments of delight is essential for building human-centered products. I wanted to ensure the team was anchored in these insights before diving into solutions.

      ### Aligning Through an Offsite

      We held an offsite at the start of the project to bring the design team together, clarify goals, and align on the initial outline of what we were building. The session began with a deep dive into prior research, presented by our UX researcher, so everyone had shared context.

      ### Research Takeaways

      The review revealed consistent themes: Walmart registry users wanted more control over their selections, clearer guidance throughout the process, and a stronger sense of trust in the overall experience.

      <CaseStudyQuote>
        "I became so frustrated in making the registry through the app, that I quit. It is not intuitive nor easy to navigate in order to add or change something."

        "It's not as user-friendly as other registries I've used. It would be better if the registry picks fell into the categories provided. That would make it much easier."
      </CaseStudyQuote>

      ### Key Insight

      Almost half of moms don't want any help creating a registry - they want to take ownership of their list and pick items that are relevant to their personal needs (GCIA Oct 2019)

      ### From Insights to Principles

      One of the most effective ways to carry research forward, not just in spirit but in practice, is through clear, actionable design principles. After reviewing the insights as a team, we distilled what we heard into a focused set of principles that would guide our decision-making throughout the project.

      ### Grounded in Real Needs

      These weren’t abstract ideals; they were rooted in the real needs, emotions, and expectations of our registry users. Our goal was to create principles that anyone on the team, product, engineering, or content could revisit and use as a north star.

      ### A Tool for Alignment and Speed

      By capturing the essence of what mattered most to our users, these principles helped us stay aligned, make faster decisions, and ensure the experience we created remained anchored in empathy and clarity.

<CaseStudySection title={"Ideation Workshop - How Might We"} subheader={"Cross-functional collaboration to prioritize opportunities"}>

      ### Turning Insights into Opportunities

      To bridge our research insights and business goals into actionable opportunities, I partnered with our staff designer to plan and facilitate a cross-functional “How Might We” workshop.

      ### A Cross-Functional Session

      We brought together eight team members from design, product, engineering, and research to explore improvements to the registry experience — using our design principles as the foundation. This collaborative exercise created alignment on where to focus and built a sense of shared ownership across disciplines as we moved into solutioning.

      ### From Ideas to Priorities

      The workshop generated more than seventy *“How Might We”* statements, reflecting a wide range of possible directions. To narrow the field, the team used dot voting to highlight the most promising ideas, then clustered them into eleven thematic categories. From there, we named and prioritized the top three opportunity areas that would guide the next phase of design. This progression, from raw brainstorming to focused priorities, gave us both a broad exploration and a clear direction forward.

<CaseStudySection title="Moving Fast in Low Fidelity" subheader="&#x22;Balancing Speed with Alignment&#x22;">
  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>
    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>
      ### Low Fidelity First

      With a tight timeline and a lean team, efficiency in our design process was critical. We began in low fidelity, using wireframes to explore ideas quickly, iterate without friction, and align on the structure of the experience before investing in polished visuals.

      ### Feedback Rhythm

      To maintain momentum, I established a focused cadence of reviews and standups. These touchpoints created space for feedback, alignment, and unblockers while keeping the team’s energy on progress rather than process.

      ### Engineering in the Loop

      As we progressed into higher fidelity, we expanded our design reviews to include engineering partners. Their input reduced late-stage churn, strengthened cross-functional alignment, and ensured the full team stayed connected to design intent as implementation began.

      ### Balanced Pace

      This rhythm created a healthy balance, fast enough to keep the project on track, but with enough structure to build clarity and confidence along the way.

      ### Schedule

      * Weekly Design Cadence
      * Mon / Wed / Fri – Leadership Reviews
      * Tue / Thur – Team Critiques
      * Daily – 15-minute standups to flag blockers and sync on progress

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>
      ### Revisiting the Research

      When we zoomed into specific enhancements, many of the most impactful updates came down to basic usability improvements and putting more control into the hands of parents-to-be. The key shift wasn’t just in the UI; it was in how we interpreted the research.

      ### Where the Previous Design Fell Short

      The earlier team had heard parents say, *“I’d like help,”* and interpreted that as a desire for the system to do the work for them. Their solution was a chatbot-driven experience that auto-generated a fully stocked registry after answering a few questions.

      ### What Parents Actually Wanted

      When we revisited the original research, even sitting down with the researcher who had conducted the interviews, we uncovered a different truth. Parents welcomed guidance in unfamiliar areas like diapers or bottles, but building a registry was a deeply personal process. They wanted to weigh advice from loved ones, make their own choices, and ultimately stay in control.

      ### Our Pivot

      Armed with this deeper understanding, we moved away from pre-populated registries. Instead, we focused on guiding parents with smart cues and applying strong usability practices to make adding items effortless. The shift supported parents through the process, rather than doing it for them.

      ### Staying on Track

      With a fixed timeline and no room for extension, alignment on priorities was critical. I partnered closely with the product and engineering directors to continually reassess scope, timelines, and effort, ensuring the team stayed focused on the highest-impact work at each stage.

      ### A Living Roadmap

      Prioritization wasn’t a one-time exercise. It was an ongoing practice of cutting, refining, and reshaping the roadmap to fit what was possible, without compromising the quality of the experience.

      ### Balancing Tradeoffs

      When deadlines required tough choices, I worked to keep the team motivated and anchored in the “why.” That meant rallying support not only around critical features, but also around small touches of polish that built trust and elevated the experience.

      ### The Outcome

      This balance of discipline and inspiration allowed us to deliver on time, while still creating an experience we were proud of, one grounded in the needs of the parents we were designing for.

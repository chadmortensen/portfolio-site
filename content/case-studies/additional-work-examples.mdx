<CaseStudySection
  title="Hands on Design Leadership"
  subheader="Creating clarity without taking over"
>
  <CaseStudyColumns layout="split">
    <CaseStudyColumn side="left">
      
      I believe design leaders have the greatest impact when they stay close enough to the work to shape it. That doesn’t mean making every design decision. It means helping the team define the right problem, creating clarity when constraints are high, and staying involved as insights become ideas, tradeoffs, and ultimately a shipped experience.
      
      ### Seeing and securing the opportunity
      
      Walmart’s recently redesigned baby registry wasn’t performing as hoped. I saw both an immediate customer problem and a strategic opportunity to build a longer-term relationship with new families. With design resources committed to other initiatives, I persuaded our design leadership to invest in the work by connecting the dots between a business opportunity and the customer experience potential. This helped me to convince and secure a focused team of four designers capable of delivering in just six weeks.
      
      ### Staying close without taking over
      I stayed close to the team as we worked on an improved experience. The existing experience assumed parents wanted a registry built for them, while the research revealed that they wanted guidance without losing control. I partnered with the staff designer and researcher to translate that insight into design principles. I then leaned in to guide exploration, and bring product, engineering, and research into the process by organizing and hosting an ideation workshop.
      
      ### Turning clarity into outcomes
      Throughout the project, I created a steady rhythm for critique, alignment, and prioritization. The redesigned registry launched on time, increasing quality registry creation by 28% and curation by 20%, with 55% of registries shared within seven days.
      
      For me, hands-on leadership is not about taking the work away from designers. It is about securing the investment, protecting the customer insight, raising the quality of the thinking, and creating the conditions for the team to turn that thinking into measurable outcomes.

    ### Building organizations where people can grow

    Growing a design team means building two things at once: the organization and the people within it. My role has included identifying the capabilities a team needs, hiring the right people, creating leadership capacity, and helping designers build meaningful careers after they join.

    ### Scaling with intention 

    At Walmart, I hired designers across multiple levels and helped build and refine the practices used to evaluate candidates. At Etsy, I hired two senior managers and supported hiring across the broader team, helping grow the organization to three senior designers and 15 additional designers. At Brightside Health, I hired a senior designer to strengthen a small team navigating a period of change. Each organization required a different approach, but the goal was consistent: build the right mix of skills, experience, and leadership for what the business and team needed next.

    ### Making growth visible

    When I joined Brightside, the design team had been operating without a leader and lacked clear expectations for their roles or how they could progress. Drawing on my experience at Etsy and Walmart, I created a career matrix defining competencies from entry-level through principal designer, along with a separate management track.

    I then worked individually with each designer to understand their strengths, development areas, and ambitions. Together, we created growth plans with specific capabilities to build and opportunities to demonstrate readiness for the next level. We revisited those plans in our coaching conversations, making growth part of everyday work instead of something discussed only during performance reviews.

    ### Preparing people for what comes next

    Through this process, I guided one senior designer into a staff designer role with confidence that they were prepared to succeed beyond their time at Brightside. For me, growing teams is not simply about adding headcount or filling open roles. It is about building an organization thoughtfully, then giving people the clarity, feedback, and opportunities they need to become more capable and confident throughout their careers.

    ### Raising quality without slowing teams down

    Scaling a design practice isn’t about adding more process. It is about creating repeatable ways for teams to prioritize the right work, learn from one another, and maintain a shared standard of quality from early thinking through implementation.

    ### Making quality a shared responsibility

    When I joined Brightside Health, designers largely worked within their individual product squads. I introduced weekly peer critiques with shared agendas, documented feedback, rotating ownership, and guidelines that grounded conversations in goals rather than personal preference. Over time, designers began sharing earlier, organizing their own feedback sessions, and co-creating across projects. In one critique, we connected similar information being designed separately for an AI-assisted clinical tool and an administrative portal, bringing the squads together to consider a shared data model that might otherwise have been missed.

    ### Scaling through clarity

    At Brightside and Walmart, I helped designers make their capacity, priorities, project sizing, and review milestones visible to their product partners. This allowed teams to make informed tradeoffs, reduced assumptions about delivery, and gave designers a stronger foundation for setting expectations. For designers or research teams that supported multiple groups, we implemented an inbound process with a queue and prioritization framework to help with expectation setting and time management. 

    ### Aligning speed, learning, and trust

    At Brightside, designers raised concerns that engineering teams were sometimes bypassing design feedback to maintain shipping velocity. I brought the issue to the senior engineering director—not as a conflict between functions, but as a conversation about what quality meant and why it mattered.

    We discovered that the goal behind velocity was learning faster, something design strongly supported. We aligned that speed should not come at the cost of customer trust and identified other ways to learn sooner, including prototypes, lighter-weight experiments, and closer design involvement as products were built. This created a shared understanding that speed and quality were not opposing goals; both were necessary to learn from something customers could trust.

    ### Building a practice that lasts

    I reinforced these habits through reusable design patterns, design systems, accessibility standards, build reviews, and broader design shareouts. Eventually, the practices extended beyond the design team: product created its own shareout, and design joined engineering’s all-hands to bring greater context and visual storytelling to the work.

    For me, a practice has truly scaled when quality no longer depends on one leader reviewing every detail. It lives in the team’s rituals, standards, partnerships, and everyday decisions.

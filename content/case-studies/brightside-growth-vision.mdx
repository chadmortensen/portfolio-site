<CaseStudySection title={"The Challenge"} subheader={"Fragmented Efforts, No Shared Vision"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      At Brightside Health, several parallel efforts were in motion to improve the intake funnel. However, they were happening as isolated experiments, each focused on short-term gains without a shared sense of direction. The designer on the squad shared some explorations he was working on.

      ### Encouragement to push the design explorations

      I encouraged the designer on the growth squad to pause and consider a more expansive approach. What if we looked beyond the immediate constraints and imagined what a truly exceptional experience could be? I often use the analogy of mild, medium, and spicy to explore beyond the most immediate solution and consider what better or exceptional solutions might be.

<CaseStudySection title={"Goals"} subheader={"Grounded decisions through analytics and insights"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      ### Business

      Brightside needed an intake experience that could increase conversion rates while enabling more flexible marketing channels.

      ### Users

      For users, the experience had to inspire confidence, helping them feel secure in their decision to seek care and giving them clarity around the cost of services.

      ### Squad

      At the squad level, success meant designing a more adaptable flow that could support a variety of user journeys while reducing friction.

      ### Leadership

      For executives, it was equally important that the work signal a long-term plan, giving confidence that near-term improvements were part of a larger, coherent vision.

<CaseStudySection title={"Competitive Audit"} subheader={"180 Steps vs. 40"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      To understand how Brightside compared to other services, I gathered input from stakeholders and conducted a scan of leading telehealth and mental health providers. Using a combination of internal suggestions and external research, I identified 11 companies to include in the audit.

      Rather than just focusing on individual screens, I mapped each company's intake journey and defined their building blocks to get a full view of the process. This helped us assess the overall structure and flow of their experiences.

      One finding stood out immediately. Brightside's intake process had 180 distinct steps. The next closest competitor had 70, and most were in the range of 20 to 40 steps.

      ### Other Key Takeaways

      - The order in which information was requested varied widely across competitors.
      - Only one other company, Hims, collected full personal and medical history during the initial sign-up. Others either postponed this step or conducted it during a follow-up.
      - About half of the competitors did not perform an insurance check early in the process, possibly due to differences in their service models.

      The main gap was clear. Brightside asked significantly more from users before they even created an account, which made the experience feel heavier and more intrusive than others in the space. This, along with strategic goals to expand marketing channels, provided a clear path to opportunities in improving the experience.

    </CaseStudyColumn>

<CaseStudySection title={"Exploration"} subheader={"Moving from Quick Fixes to Big Leaps"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      Unlike many vision projects, this effort did not begin with a structured group brainstorm. The designer had already started working on near-term improvements. My goal was to help him think more broadly and explore directions that weren't limited by what could be built in the next sprint.

      Instead of formal workshops, we used a series of design critiques and 1-on-1s to push the work further. I challenged him to integrate new insights from the research and bring bolder, more ambitious options into the designs.

      ### Two Standout Ideas Emerged

      - Use AI to offer helpful guidance during complex decision points, such as selecting a payment method or understanding insurance. We knew there were moments of overwhelm, which caused friction.
      - Introduce a more human tone by incorporating videos and personalized therapist content, making the process feel more like the kinds of conversations people were accustomed to offline and less like a transaction.

      Through critiques, we evaluated which ideas could be combined, simplified, or expanded. This iterative approach helped us shape a stronger, more inspiring vision.

<CaseStudySection title={"The Design Vision"} subheader={"More Human, More Assistive, More Concise"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      The design vision focused on three guiding principles:

      ### Be more assistive and provide clearer answers

      - While AI was not the core of the vision, it played a valuable role in making the experience feel smarter and more responsive. It surfaced relevant content at the right time and used natural language to reduce friction. I wanted to be careful and not insert AI for the sake of using a new technology. It needed to serve a purpose.

      ### Keep it simple and concise

      - We removed unnecessary steps, combined related screens, and postponed some questions until after the user created an account. This helped make the experience shorter and less mentally taxing.

      ### Make it more personal and human-centered

      - We aimed to create a sign-up process that felt supportive and empathetic. Video content helped explain complicated decisions. We also highlighted therapist bios and working styles to create a stronger emotional connection between patient and provider.

<CaseStudySection title={"Navigating Stakeholder Concerns"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      When we proposed shortening the sign-up process, we quickly ran into pushback from our clinical leadership. Their concern was valid: reducing the number of upfront questions might make the process easier for prospective members, but it could also leave therapists underprepared for their first session.

      ### Finding the Right Balance

      To address this, we partnered closely with product management and the clinical team to review the intake questions one by one. Together, we identified which were truly essential to begin care, which were “nice to have,” and which had become outdated. This collaborative process helped reduce friction without compromising clinical readiness.

      ### Testing Assumptions

      To build confidence in the approach, we ran a pilot test that removed all personal and medical history questions from the initial sign-up. Therapists instead asked these during the first session. The result? Sign-ups didn’t increase enough to justify the added burden on therapists. This experiment provided valuable evidence that simply deferring all questions wasn’t the right solution, but that a more balanced reduction was possible.

      ### Continuing the Evolution

      The team is still exploring ways to streamline data collection. Possibilities include capturing information in the member dashboard or a virtual waiting room before the first session. Looking ahead, AI could help further reduce the load; for example, by summarizing responses into a concise report that therapists can review right before meeting a new member.

<CaseStudySection title={"Output"} subheader={"Prototype & Narrative to Inspire"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      The final output included a functional prototype of the redesigned intake experience. Built in Figma, it allowed stakeholders to click through and experience the flow firsthand. It was also embedded in a Figma Slides presentation to provide context and narrative.

      The prototype was shared with the head of product, the growth squad, and the CEO. The CEO's response was brief but encouraging. He said it was clear that the work was thoughtful and asked when the new experience could be implemented.

      More importantly, the prototype provided a concrete vision. It gave the team something to rally around and helped connect near-term initiatives with long-term goals.

<CaseStudySection title={"Results"} subheader={"A Clearer Path Forward"}>

  <CaseStudyColumns layout={"split"}>

    <CaseStudyColumn side={"left"}>

      While the full vision has not yet been implemented, it has already begun to shape the team’s direction.

      - **Validated the roadmap** – The work confirmed that the squad’s short-term initiatives were aligned with a larger, more ambitious goal.
      - **Strengthened cross-functional trust** – The collaboration with clinical leadership was a turning point. By listening to their concerns, testing assumptions, and deciding together which data was essential, we built credibility and demonstrated that design could reconcile business, user, and clinical needs.
      - **Elevated design’s role** – The project reinforced the value of design as a strategic partner, not just a delivery function.
      - **Improved clarity for engineering** – The head of engineering noted he now understood more clearly *why* the team was pursuing its roadmap, which boosted collaboration and focus.
      - **Fostered growth in the squad designer** – He shifted from solving isolated problems to thinking systemically and aspirationally, moving from *what’s feasible* to *what’s possible*.

      ### Personal reflection

      This alignment across design, product, and clinical teams wasn’t just about reducing sign-up steps; it showed that when you bring different perspectives together, you can unlock solutions that serve everyone. That’s the kind of trust and shared vision that makes real transformation sustainable.
